Which model should we run?

What to run on your own GPUs, and what it will really cost.

The answer above comes from what you enter below, and updates as you type. It ranks the models that actually clear your quality and latency bars, and prices each one at your volume — including the capacity you pay for and do not use. The scores are yours to supply: no benchmark ships with this.

1 · The job

Picking one fills in the shape of the job below — how much goes in, how much comes back, how fast it has to answer, how many run at once. They are starting points from typical deployments, not measurements of yours: overwrite any of them and this switches to Custom.

Busiest hour is how much busier your peak is than your average — hardware has to be bought for the peak and is paid for all year. 1 means traffic is flat around the clock, which almost nothing is; office-hours traffic is usually 2× to 5×. It is left at 1 because guessing your traffic shape is not this tool's job, but leaving it there will understate what you need to buy.

The quality bar is the only number nobody can supply for you. It means: on 100 real examples a domain expert has graded, what score is good enough to ship?

2 · The hardware

Advanced — batching, power and overheads

Overheads cover rack, network, support contract and admin time — not electricity, which PUE already accounts for. Idle draw matters: a GPU sitting idle still costs nearly what a busy one does, which is the whole reason the two cost figures below differ.

3 · The candidates

Model Total (B) Active (B) Precision Your score (%) Speed (tok/s) KV MB/token API $/M in API $/M out

API prices are yours to enter. Nothing is hardcoded here — published prices change monthly and any figure baked into this file would be wrong by the time you read it. Take the two numbers off the provider's pricing page for the model you would actually rent. The comparison covers tokens only: egress, rate-limit headroom and the fact that your data leaves the building are not priced.

KV MB/token is the attention cache each sequence carries, and it is what makes high concurrency and long context expensive in VRAM: at your settings it is multiplied by concurrency and by input plus output length. Take it from the model card — 2 × layers × kv-heads × head-dim × bytes, in megabytes. Left empty it is not counted at all, and the GPU count below is a floor rather than an answer.

Total is every parameter the weights contain; active is how many are used per token. For a dense model they are the same number. For a mixture-of-experts they are not, and the difference decides everything: the VRAM bill — and so the GPU count — follows the total, while speed follows the active part. A model can therefore need eight GPUs to hold and still answer quickly.

Your score is the model's result on your graded examples for this job — not a public benchmark, and not its score on a different task. A model that summarises well may write poor code, and the ranking below is only as good as the match between your test set and the work you picked above. Speed auto-fills with a rough estimate from memory bandwidth; it is a shortlisting guess that can be wrong by half. Type a measured number over it as soon as you have one, and it will be marked as measured.

4 · Sharing the hardware

Everything sharing the box is assumed to run the same model on the same deployment. Cost is split by how much of the capacity each side actually consumes, so your share falls as the box fills up. If the other work needs a different model, it needs a different box and this will flatter you.

Estimated speeds assume the model is memory-bandwidth-bound during generation, which is the usual case, with batching gains that flatten as concurrency rises. VRAM figures cover weights plus roughly 20% runtime overhead and exclude the KV cache, which grows with concurrency and context length — so treat a model that only just fits as one that doesn't. Nothing here is a substitute for running the thing on your own box for an afternoon.
